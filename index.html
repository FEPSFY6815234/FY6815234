Abstract

      This article explains the information scientists needed for scientists to estimate the age of the universe such as absorbtion spectrum of stars and redshifting. Then shows how you can use the expansion of the universe
       to estimate the universe's age. It then explains how the age of the universe is only an estimation as the rate of expansion of the universe has changed over time due to factors such as gravity and dark energy.

Introduction

      The age of the universe is estimated to be around 13.8 billion years old (NASA, 2026) although the exact age is unknown. The first step towards this estimation is knowing that the atmosphere of stars contains different elements.
      Each specific element, such as oxygen or hydrogen, will absorb unique wavelengths of light (BBC, 2026). An absortion spectum can then be used which shows which wavelengths of light are missing,
      appearing as a black line in the spectum. However, when you analyse light of stars from distant galaxies, the pattern of black lines looks the same. The only difference being each line is shifted towards 
      the red end of the spectrum (BBC, 2026). Since waveslength on this end of the spectrum are longer this shows that space itself is expanding and light waves are stretched with it. This is known as a cosmological red shift. (CGP, 2018, p.188)
      
      Now that scientists knew the universe is expanding, finding the rate of expansion would allow scientists to estimate the age of the universe. This is because the units of the rate of expansion of the universe would be velocity/distance.
      This can be rewritten as distance/(time*distance) which simplifies to 1/time. This means the reciprocal of the rate of expansion would show the time taken for the universe to expand to its current size, which is 
      the same amount of time as the age of the universe (CPG, 2018, p.192). The unit of measurement that discribes the rate the universe is expanding is known as the Hubble's constant. The problem is that scientists disagree on its exact value.
      The generally accepted values is that H0 is between 65 and 80 kms^-1 with most scientists estimating it is in the mid to low 70s (CPG, 2018, p.192). 
       
       Another issue is that this assumes the expansion of the universe is constant over time.
      Until about 5 billion years ago, the expansion of the universe was likely decelerating due to gravity attracting the mass of the universe together. In the present, there is evidence that the expansion of the universe
      is accellerating, due to dark energy. Scientists are unsure what exactly dark energy even is (CGP, 2018, p.192).
